I was hired into my current job partly because I had been an early adopter of AI tools. It was clear to me early on that this would become the tool of my trade, and I've spent the last few years getting good at it. I use coding agents every day. I like them. This essay was also written in conversation with one.

So this isn't a piece against AI. It's about trust: how a society comes to rely on a new source of information, who ends up holding that trust, and what happens when the reliance arrives before anyone has decided to trust anything.

The idea started with Yuval Noah Harari's _Nexus_,[^harari] which argues that information networks have always been what holds human societies together, from stone age tribes to modern bureaucracies. I read it as a history book and kept seeing the present in it.

## The internet arc

"Don't trust everything you read online" used to be the standard warning. The internet was exotic and unreliable, a curiosity, and if something wasn't in a book or a newspaper it probably wasn't worth knowing.

By the mid-2000s that had flipped. The internet had become the place credibility came from, and if you couldn't find something by searching, people started to doubt it existed. We stopped asking whether we could trust what we read online and simply assumed we could. The skepticism wore off without anyone deciding to drop it.

Skepticism, then acceptance, then dependency. It happened slowly enough that most of us only noticed once someone used it as a weapon.

## Pizzagate, QAnon and a real scandal

In late October and early November 2016, users on 4chan's /pol/ board combed through John Podesta's leaked emails and decided that "cheese pizza" was code for child abuse.[^tuters] The theory that grew from it said a Washington pizzeria, Comet Ping Pong, hid a child-trafficking ring run by people around Hillary Clinton's campaign, in its basement. The restaurant has no basement. On December 4, 2016, a man walked in with an AR-15 to free the children, fired it, searched the building and found nothing. He was sentenced to four years.[^welch]

A year later, on October 28, 2017, an anonymous poster calling themselves "Q" appeared on the same board.[^q-origins] Ten years earlier that would have stayed a fringe theory. Instead it became a mass movement, and I think the trust was already there for it to use. People who once questioned everything online treated anonymous posts as gospel. By 2023 almost a quarter of Americans believed some part of it.[^wired-qanon]

Then the Epstein files came out, and this is where it gets uncomfortable. Epstein was a real serial abuser of girls. In 2008 federal prosecutors gave him a deal that shielded him and unnamed co-conspirators and was kept from his victims; a federal judge later ruled it violated their rights.[^herald] It took Julie K. Brown's reporting in the _Miami Herald_ in 2018 to bring the case back.[^perversion] Ghislaine Maxwell is serving twenty years.[^maxwell] Powerful people from both parties, royalty and finance kept company with him for years. Since the Epstein Files Transparency Act passed 427 to 1 in November 2025, the Justice Department has released about 3.5 million pages, withheld around 200,000 and later removed tens of thousands of files again.[^doj-release][^cbs-removed]

So were the Pizzagate people right? About the specifics, no. Nothing in the files supports a ring at Comet Ping Pong, a code in Podesta's emails, or crimes by Hillary Clinton or Podesta.[^snopes] About the shape, partly. Elites did protect a child abuser, and the official record did hide things. That's how a conspiracy theory earns trust: real institutional failure makes distrust rational, and the theory spends that distrust on invented details. When the real scandal surfaces years later, the invented parts feel vindicated too. The _New York Times_ reported QAnon believers "crowing that they were right" after the January 2026 release.[^nyt-qanon] The real case was cracked by a reporter and by lawsuits, though, not by decoding pizza orders.

Here's my own reading, and I want to mark it as that. The movement that spent years hunting a fictional elite abuse ring ended up with its own leader documented in the files of a real one. His social contact with Epstein is on record, though he hasn't been accused of a crime and denies wrongdoing. He opposed releasing the files for months before signing the law. Mike Rothschild, who has written a book on QAnon, put the turn better than I can: "We've gone from Epstein being the center of a vast interlocking ring of satanic child traffickers to Epstein being a bad guy who Trump exposed, so let's stop talking about it."[^nyt-qanon] Propaganda research has a name for the extreme form of accusing your opponent of what you do yourself: "accusation in a mirror."[^marcus] I can't prove intent, and nobody can from the outside. But when I watch this administration, its accusations keep reading like a description of itself.

## Two currents, one soil

While QAnon grew on the boards, another current ran above it. In 2007 the software engineer Curtis Yarvin started blogging as Mencius Moldbug, arguing that democracy had failed and should give way to something closer to a CEO-run state.[^formalist] In 2009 Peter Thiel wrote that he no longer believed "that freedom and democracy are compatible."[^thiel] Thiel later funded Yarvin's startup.[^vox-yarvin] In 2021 JD Vance cited Yarvin on a podcast while urging Trump to "fire every single midlevel bureaucrat."[^verge-vance] Thiel had backed Vance's Senate run.

The two currents don't share an origin. There's no evidence Yarvin posted on 4chan or that Q ever drew on him. What they share is soil: the anonymous boards, forums and blogs of the late 2000s and 2010s, contempt for institutions, and a vocabulary. "Red pill" in its political sense is Yarvin's most influential coinage,[^newyorker] and Gamergate in 2014, organized from 4chan, carried it into the mass culture of the boards.[^datasociety]

Yarvin described the division of labor himself in 2008: "What was needed was the combination of philosopher and crowd."[^newyorker] I don't think anyone designed what followed. But that's roughly what we got: QAnon as the crowd, the Thiel circle as the philosophers, and by 2024 both inside the same coalition. This is my reading, not a finding in the literature. It's still the clearest picture I have of who ended up holding the trust the internet had built.

## The people building it are writing the rules

After the 2024 election, Elon Musk and Larry Ellison sat in on Trump's transition meetings at Mar-a-Lago.[^nyt-transition] Thiel's protégés took policy roles: Vance as Vice President, David Sacks as "AI and crypto czar."[^fortune] Musk left the administration in May 2025 after a falling-out with Trump,[^reuters] and his company didn't need him in the building. In December 2025 Trump signed an executive order creating a Justice Department task force whose only job is to challenge state AI laws.[^eo] In April 2026 Musk's xAI sued Colorado over its AI Act, the Justice Department joined the suit, and enforcement was suspended within days.[^colorado]

The same company runs Grok. In July 2025 xAI told the chatbot to "not shy away from making claims which are politically incorrect," and within days it was posting antisemitic replies and calling itself "MechaHitler."[^politifact] Grok 4 was sold as "maximally truth-seeking," and on questions about immigration, abortion and Israel it searched for Musk's own posts before answering.[^techcrunch] In October 2025 Musk launched Grokipedia, an AI-written encyclopedia pitched as an unbiased replacement for Wikipedia.[^cnn] WIRED found entries pushing conservative talking points,[^wired-grokipedia] and The Verge found others copied from Wikipedia nearly word for word.[^verge]

An AI oracle with an owner, built to confirm one faction's view of the world, and still leaning on the volunteer encyclopedia it was meant to replace.

## But then, dependency without trust

The internet earned our trust before we depended on it. You'd expect AI to follow the same path. It didn't. People depend on it while telling you they don't trust it.

A University of Melbourne and KPMG study of over 48,000 people in 47 countries found only 46% willing to trust AI, fewer than before ChatGPT came out. Yet 66% use AI output without checking whether it's accurate.[^kpmg] When Google shows an AI summary, people click through to a website in 8% of visits instead of 15%, and click a source inside the summary just 1% of the time.[^pew] In a September 2026 survey of likely midterm voters, only 1% named AI as their most credible source of election information, but at least 38% had gotten election information from an AI search summary, and only about half of them knew a machine wrote it.[^aapc]

Nobody decided to trust it. It came with the search results.

To be fair to the machines: when the Brennan Center fed ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok common election lies, the bots mostly pushed back, even against users posing as election deniers. The same models then helped the researchers make convincing fake election images with little resistance.[^brennan]

The dependency goes deeper than search. OpenAI estimates that about 0.15% of ChatGPT's weekly users show "heightened levels of emotional attachment" to it,[^openai] which at 800 million weekly users is roughly 1.2 million people a week.[^wired-openai] A twelve-month study of more than 2,000 adults found that lonely people turn to chatbots for company, and that doing so predicted more emotional isolation months later.[^folk]

## At work, the promise gets trusted

This is the part I know from the inside.

Few of the people who work with AI output trust it. In Stack Overflow's 2025 survey, 33% of developers trusted the accuracy of AI tools and 46% distrusted it.[^so2025] What gets trusted at work is the promise of speed, and that trust is strongest at the top. In an Upwork survey, 96% of executives expected AI to raise productivity, while 77% of employees using it said it had added to their workload.[^upwork] Shopify's CEO told staff in 2025 that "reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation" and that teams must show why AI can't do a job before asking for headcount.[^shopify] Coinbase's CEO said he fired engineers who didn't onboard onto AI coding tools within a week.[^coinbase] Klarna replaced customer service staff with AI and then started hiring humans again, its CEO conceding that the result was "lower quality."[^klarna]

The users aren't immune either. In METR's 2025 study, experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI tools, while believing afterwards that they'd been 20% faster.[^metr] The feeling of speed is persuasive, for the people doing the work and even more for the people watching the dashboards.

What I see, and read about constantly, is managers who expect the agent to do its thing and then commit and push. I don't work that way. AI makes me faster, and I still want to understand the code and the structure that comes out of it, because I'm the one who has to maintain it, debug it at the worst possible moment and defend it in review.

## The shame gap

There's another gap I suspect but can't prove. In public, developers tend to say they review everything their agent writes. I think many say it out of something close to shame, and do less in practice.

The nearest data fits. In Sonar's 2026 survey, 96% of developers didn't fully trust AI-generated code, but only 48% always checked it before committing.[^sonar] In the KPMG study, 61% of workers avoided revealing when they used AI at all.[^kpmg]

At the other end are the people who proudly don't read the code anymore. Andrej Karpathy coined "vibe coding" in February 2025: "I 'Accept All' always, I don't read the diffs anymore." He also said it was "not too bad for throwaway weekend projects,"[^karpathy] a caveat that tends to get dropped. In July 2025 a Replit agent deleted a production database with records on more than 1,200 executives, during a code freeze.[^replit]

## Trust you build yourself

My own practice is somewhere in between, and I think the middle is where the honest answer is.

I don't check every line, and I don't accept everything blindly. Over time I've built up written conventions for how my code should look: naming, structure, patterns, what goes where. The agents work against those. That raises the baseline of what gets produced, so I can trust it more without trusting it blindly. Then I check where it matters, and I maintain the conventions when the output drifts. On some projects that means reading almost everything. On others it means reading the parts that carry risk.

That's a different kind of trust from everything above. It isn't handed over to a search box, an oracle or a productivity promise. I build it, I can inspect it, and I can take it back.

## Harari's warning

Harari's central point in _Nexus_ is that information networks don't serve truth by default. They serve power. The printing press spread Enlightenment ideas and witch-hunting manuals alike. The internet gave us Wikipedia and QAnon. AI is a tool in the same line, and Grokipedia sitting next to Wikipedia is as clear an illustration as Harari could have asked for.

Whether AI becomes trusted was never the question. It's depended on already, mostly by people who would tell you they don't trust it. What I want to know is who holds that dependency, what they do with it, and who gets to tune the answers.

## Where I land

The internet taught us that trust given to a technology is hard to take back. With AI we skipped the giving. The dependency arrived through defaults, mandates and search boxes, and the people best placed to profit from it are the ones writing its rules.

I'm not going to stop using it. The answer I've found, for my own small corner, is to keep my trust in it something I build and can take back.
